The problem

Every switch feels the same.

Indian modular switchboards are rows of identical rockers. In a hostel guest room, a PG or a relative's home, a blind or low-vision person cannot tell the fan from the light without help or trial and error. Smart switches need an app, braille needs literacy most late-blind adults don't have, and plain bump dots mark a spot but carry no meaning.

How it works

The AI sees where. People decide what.

A photo can show where the switches are, but never what they control: that's hidden in the wiring. So the AI does the visible part and says when it is unsure, and the human does the rest.

1

One photo

A caregiver photographs the board on a phone or laptop. Nothing leaves the device's local network.

2

AI finds the switches

A two-stage detector straightens the plate, finds and numbers S1…Sn, and labels each box fairly sure unsure or guess, with a reason.

3

Human checks and decides

Unsure boxes must be opened one by one. The caregiver fixes boxes, flips each switch, chooses what it controls, and ticks "I flipped it and checked".

4

Stickers + guide

A printable English / हिंदी guide says which sticker goes on which switch. After that: no phone, no internet, no power. Just touch.

The tactile code

Four stickers you can buy, not print.

Everything is off the shelf: clear bump stickers and a hook-and-loop square. We avoid a bar because ISO 24503 gives a tactile bar the meaning "stop". The code is refined from users' touch errors: any sticker below 90% recognition in our pilot is swapped by refine_vocabulary.py.

Learn four markers once and they mean the same thing on every SPARSH-labelled board. A plain switch means "unused or unknown".

The human-AI question

Does showing the AI's confidence help people catch its mistakes?

A wrong label is invisible to a blind user, so the installer must catch the AI's errors. In Study B, installers review board photos twice: once with bare AI suggestions, once with confidence, reasons and forced review. In both, about a quarter of the AI's boxes are deliberately wrong, following the course's trust-calibration brief.
